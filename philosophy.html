Teaching philosophy

Learning

I believe students learn best when they are active participants in their learning; when they understand what they are learning, can connect new ideas to what they already know, and have opportunities to explore, practise, question and apply their understanding in meaningful ways.

My role as a teacher is to provide both structure and possibility. I explicitly teach and model the knowledge and skills students need, provide appropriate scaffolding and feedback, and gradually create greater opportunities for independence, choice and creative decision-making. I want students to develop the confidence to move beyond asking “Is this right?” towards thinking “What could I try next?”

I value learning that is purposeful and connected. My experience teaching across the full primary curriculum has shown me how powerful learning can be when students recognise connections between ideas rather than experiencing subjects in isolation. Within Visual Arts, this might mean exploring geometry through isometric drawing, science through observational illustration, literacy through typography and visual communication, or sustainability through materials and collaborative art making.

I also believe learning should involve experimentation, challenge and revision. Students need opportunities to try something, encounter difficulty, reconsider their approach and try again. Mistakes are not something to avoid, but an important part of developing knowledge, skills, resilience and independence.

Ultimately, I want students to leave my classroom with more than a completed piece of work. I want them to have developed skills they can apply, ideas they can build upon, confidence in their own capabilities and curiosity about what they might learn next.

Wellbeing

I believe wellbeing is at the heart of learning. Students are better able to engage, take risks and thrive when they feel safe, supported and genuinely valued. Every child brings their own experiences, strengths and perspectives to the classroom, and these should be recognised and respected.

Positive relationships are the foundation of my practice. I aim to know my students as individuals, meet them at their point of need and create a strong sense of belonging where every child knows they are an important member of our classroom community. I believe every child deserves equitable access to meaningful learning opportunities, regardless of their abilities, additional needs, language background or starting point. Through inclusive, differentiated teaching, I strive to ensure all students can participate, experience success and recognise their own growth and achievements.

My studies in child and adolescent counselling, mental health and self-esteem development have strengthened my understanding of students as whole people, not simply learners. By creating a culture of trust, inclusion and care, I want students to feel confident to express themselves, connect with others and grow both personally and academically.

Critical thinking

I believe education should develop more than knowledge and skills; it should empower students to become curious, thoughtful and independent thinkers who question, challenge assumptions, solve problems and take ownership of their learning and personal growth.

Critical thinking encourages students to look beyond the obvious, explore different perspectives and make informed decisions rather than simply accepting information at face value. Metacognition builds an awareness of their own thinking and learning processes, helping students recognise their strengths, identify challenges, evaluate their choices and consider how they can improve.

I encourage students to ask questions, experiment, take creative risks and embrace mistakes as opportunities for learning. By fostering self-reflection, resilience and a growth mindset, I aim to help students develop confidence in their own judgement, recognise their capacity to influence outcomes and understand that progress requires effort, adaptability and personal responsibility.

Ultimately, I want students to see themselves as active participants in their education, not passive recipients of information. These skills extend far beyond the classroom, empowering young people to navigate challenges, make thoughtful decisions and contribute meaningfully to the world around them. Learning how to think, rather than simply what to think, is fundamental to becoming a capable, reflective and independent person.
